What this shows:
Merge commits change who the top contributor is. 25 of melissa.iori's 85 commits are "Merge pull request" commits. They record who clicked merge, not who wrote code. Exclude merges from authorship metrics; count them separately as review activity.
Identity resolution changes the bus factor. The unresolved identity is almost certainly "nathan" vs "nathan.tallman" (1 commit). Merging them moves bus factor 50% from 2 to 1. Small teams sit right on these integer thresholds, so the metric is fragile.
12/12 months overstates continuity. The same repo was active in only 28 of 52 weeks.
The near-identical issue medians (2,324.809 h vs 2,324.812 h) are almost certainly the same event. A gap of about 11 seconds means the "first response" was the comment posted while closing the issue. Exclude comments made within 60 seconds of closure by the closer, or count closure itself as a response — but not both.
61 of 67 issues unanswered is likely an artefact. In a 4–5 person team, most issues are internal tickets filed by maintainers. A "response from someone other than the author" will rarely come. Report the share of issues opened by maintainers vs outside users before interpreting.
12 of 27 PRs "unanswered" while 21 were merged means self-merges without comments. Count a merge or close by someone other than the author as a response.
The 91.30% acceptance ratio (21/23) is arithmetically correct.

Revised Component 4 metric set
Recommended: 13 weighted metrics in four dimensions — Continuity 35%, Contributors 25%, Responsiveness 35%, Adoption 5%. Every metric below was tested or is a standard GitHub API field. Scores use fixed anchors, not sample percentiles, so a repo's score does not move when other repos are added or dropped.
Common rules: window 1 Oct 2025 00:00 UTC (inclusive) to 1 Oct 2026 00:00 UTC (exclusive); default branch; author date; bots removed; merge commits excluded from authorship; identities resolved by email + GitHub login.

Reported but not weighted (diagnostics): median time to first response with n, acceptance ratio with merged / closed / open counts, bus factor 50% and 80%, open issues older than 180 days, bot share, merge-commit count, share of issues opened by maintainers.
Why these thresholds: issue and PR cohorts end early enough that every item in them has the full 14, 7, 30 or 90 days observable before the cutoff. This removes the open-item censoring problem without guessing. Metrics 8–11 need at least 10 eligible items; below that they are "insufficient data", never zero.
Provisional APTrust result: the seven git-based metrics (60% of weight) give 32.6 weighted points, i.e. 54/100 on the available weight. This is an illustration only; do not report it until metrics 8–13 are pulled.
The weights remain a judgement. Test them against equal weights and against dropping each dimension in turn, as section 9 of your draft already proposes.
Missing data policy and next steps
Score a repo only when at least 75% of total weight and at least half of each dimension's weight are available; otherwise report "insufficient data". When the threshold is met, renormalise over the available weight and publish the coverage percentage beside the score.
Dormant is a result, not missing data. A repo with zero human commits in the window scores 0 on metrics 1–3, not "unavailable".
External trackers: pull issues from the real tracker (pkp-lib, Jira, separate Issues repos) where it has an API; otherwise mark metrics 8 and 10 unavailable and flag the repo.
Non-GitHub hosts: GitLab and Gitea expose equivalent issue and merge-request data; Bitbucket often has issues disabled.

Step 4 — bot and identity rules
Bots: GitHub account type "Bot" is authoritative. Otherwise 31 named accounts (dependabot, renovate, github-actions, pre-commit-ci, weblate, copilot…) plus a pattern list. Bot commits are counted separately, never as contributors.
Identity: merged within one repo only, in this order: the repo's .mailmap; same e-mail; GitHub no-reply e-mail login; GitHub API login; identical full name of two or more words (flagged for audit); your manual map. Never on first names, initials or similar names.
Forks: commits inherited from the upstream project are excluded (Oasisbr).
Umbrella repos: a product split across repos is scored as a set, with commits de-duplicated.
The full-name rule merges APTrust's two "Nathan Tallman" identities. This is slightly looser than rule 7 in your draft, which allowed no name-based merges; keep it or switch it off in rules.py.

Step 6 — sensitivity and correlation (90 repos, commit metrics)
Weights barely matter. Equal metric weights or equal dimension weights give rank correlation 0.99 with the proposed weights. The top 10 are identical, and the median repo moves 1–2 places.
Two pairs are redundant among active repos: human commits vs active weeks (ρ = 0.87) and contributors vs effective contributors (ρ = 0.88). Retention also tracks contributors (ρ = 0.83).
The commit metrics measure one thing: overall activity. Real differentiation will come from metrics 8–11. Re-run analysis.py after the API pull before finalising weights.
Large projects hit the caps. Galaxy, OJS, GeoServer, VuFind and DSpace all score 99–100, so the anchors cannot separate the largest projects. That is acceptable if the index measures sustainability thresholds rather than size.
